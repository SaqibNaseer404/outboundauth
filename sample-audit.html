Findings, in plain English

Severity: High

1. No DMARC record — the domain can be impersonated

DMARC is the record that tells Gmail, Outlook, and Yahoo: "if an email claims to be from us but fails the checks, block it — and tell us about it." This domain doesn't have one.

What that means for the business: a scammer can send invoices, quote requests, or "updated bank details" emails that genuinely appear to come from this company. Customers can't tell the difference, the company never sees it happening, and one incident can burn years of trust. It also means the domain doesn't meet the sender requirements Google and Yahoo now apply to business senders.

Severity: High

2. DKIM is not switched on — email isn't being signed

DKIM adds a tamper-proof digital signature to every outgoing email. This domain sends through Microsoft 365, which supports DKIM — but the DNS records that turn it on were never published, so mail goes out unsigned.

What that means for the business: inbox providers trust signed mail more. Unsigned mail from a domain with no DMARC is the weakest possible combination — it looks exactly like the setup scammers use.

Severity: None — good news

3. SPF is done properly

The SPF record authorises Microsoft 365 and nothing else, ends in a hard fail (-all), and uses only 1 DNS lookup out of the 10 allowed. No changes needed here — this is the record most small businesses get wrong, and this one is right.

Severity: None — good news

4. No blacklist problems

The mail server IP is clean on Spamhaus ZEN, SpamCop, and Barracuda. Deliverability trouble for this domain is an authentication problem, not a reputation problem — which makes it fully fixable.

The fix plan

These are the exact records we would publish (shown with the sample domain). Fixing this domain takes two DNS records and one switch inside Microsoft 365 — this is precisely what the $149 full setup does for you, or you can hand this plan to whoever manages your DNS.

Step 1 — Turn on DKIM. Publish these two CNAME records, then enable DKIM for the domain in the Microsoft 365 admin portal (the portal shows the exact target hostnames for your account):

selector1._domainkey.clientdomain.com
  → selector1-clientdomain-com._domainkey.<your-tenant>.onmicrosoft.com

selector2._domainkey.clientdomain.com
  → selector2-clientdomain-com._domainkey.<your-tenant>.onmicrosoft.com

Step 2 — Add DMARC in watch mode first. This changes nothing about delivery — it just starts sending you reports of who is sending mail using your domain:

_dmarc.clientdomain.com  (TXT)
v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@clientdomain.com; fo=1

Step 3 — Enforce, once the reports are clean (usually 2–4 weeks). Move the policy up in stages — quarantine, then reject — so fake mail gets blocked:

_dmarc.clientdomain.com  (TXT)
v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@clientdomain.com; fo=1

SPF: no change. The existing record stays exactly as it is.

What "fixed" looks like

After these fixes and a re-check: DKIM signing every outgoing email, DMARC reports arriving daily, and a p=reject policy telling every major inbox provider to refuse impersonation attempts. A domain in that state would be expected to score in the 90s on this same audit — and, more importantly, its customers could trust that an email from the company really is from the company.
