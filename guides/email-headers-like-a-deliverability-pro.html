How to read email headers like a deliverability pro

Every email carries a hidden flight recorder: the full headers. They reveal the SPF, DKIM and DMARC verdicts, who really sent the message, and every server it passed through. Once you can read them, spam-folder mysteries stop being mysteries.

How do I see full email headers?

Email clients hide headers by default — you only see From, To, Subject and Date. To see the rest:

  • Gmail: open the message, click the three-dot menu, then Show original. Copy the full text from the new window.
  • Outlook (web): open the message, click the three-dot menu, then View → View message details.
  • Outlook (desktop): double-click the message to open it, then File → Properties → Internet headers.
  • Apple Mail: select the message, then View → Message → All Headers (or Raw Source for the complete text).

Headers read bottom-up: the first server in the chain is at the bottom, the last one before your inbox is at the top.

Which headers actually matter?

A full header dump can be a hundred lines long. Deliverability pros focus on a short list:

  • Authentication-Results — the receiving server's official verdict on SPF, DKIM and DMARC. Start here.
  • Return-Path — the envelope sender, used for SPF checks. Often differs from the visible From.
  • DKIM-Signature — shows the signing domain in its d= tag (e.g. d=google.com).
  • Received — one line per server hop, each with IP, hostname and timestamp. Together they trace the delivery path.
  • Message-ID — a unique fingerprint for the message.
  • Reply-To — where replies actually go; if it differs from From, treat the message with care.
  • List-Unsubscribe — present on legitimate marketing mail; its absence on bulk-looking mail is a warning sign.
  • X-Spam-Status / X-Spam-Score — the receiving filter's spam verdict and score, when exposed.

How do I read an Authentication-Results line?

This line is the verdict that decides inbox or spam. It looks like this:

Authentication-Results: mx.example.net;
  spf=pass (sender IP is 203.0.113.45) smtp.mailfrom=example.com;
  dkim=pass header.d=example.com;
  dmarc=pass header.from=example.com

Each result is one of:

  • pass — the check succeeded. All three passing with aligned domains is the gold standard.
  • fail — the check failed. dmarc=fail is what pushes mail toward spam or rejection.
  • softfail — SPF failed but the record ended in ~all (soft-fail), so the receiver notes it without acting hard. A sign the record needs tightening — see the SPF guide.
  • none — no check was possible, usually because the domain has no published record.
  • temperror / permerror — the lookup itself broke (DNS timeout, or too many lookups for SPF).

When mail lands in spam, this line tells you exactly which check failed and for which domain — no guessing needed.

How do I tell who really sent this email?

The From header is just text — anyone can write anything in it. The real identity check is a three-way comparison:

  1. From: what the recipient sees.
  2. Return-Path: the envelope domain that SPF validates.
  3. d= in DKIM-Signature: the domain that cryptographically signed the message.

If all three agree on your domain and dmarc=pass, the message is authentically yours. If the From says your domain but DKIM's d= says something else entirely, that's spoofing or an unauthorised sender — exactly what DMARC enforcement is designed to stop.

How do I trace the path an email took?

Read the Received lines from bottom to top. Each one names a server, its IP, and the time it handled the message. Look for:

  • Unexpected hops — relays in countries or networks that have nothing to do with your sending service.
  • Time gaps — long delays between hops point to greylisting or a congested relay.
  • IP mismatches — a hop claiming a hostname whose IP doesn't belong to your sending provider.

For your own campaigns, the first hop after your sending platform should be a server you recognise — Google's, your SMTP provider's, or your cold-email tool's.

What do spam-score headers mean?

Some servers add lines like X-Spam-Status: Yes, score=7.2 or X-Spam-Level: ******* (one asterisk per point). A score above the server's threshold — commonly 5 — means spam. The detail lines often name the triggered rules, such as missing authentication or suspicious URLs, which tells you what to fix.
